FOUNDATION

Vocabulary: Synonyms, Antonyms and Idioms

Detailed concept notes
1. Synonyms and antonyms

A synonym is a word with a similar meaning, while an antonym expresses an opposite meaning. The correct choice depends on the context in which a word is used.

For example, abundant means plentiful, while scarce means limited or insufficient.

2. Word roots, prefixes and suffixes

Word roots carry the central meaning of a word. Prefixes appear before a root and suffixes appear after it. Understanding them helps you work out unfamiliar vocabulary.

  • Un-: not or opposite, as in unhappy.
  • Pre-: before, as in preview.
  • -ology: study of, as in biology.
  • -less: without, as in careless.
3. Idioms and phrases

An idiom is an established expression whose meaning cannot always be understood from its individual words. Learn its meaning along with a sentence showing how it is used.

  • Break the ice: begin a friendly conversation.
  • Once in a blue moon: very rarely.
  • At the eleventh hour: at the last possible moment.
4. One-word substitutions

A one-word substitution replaces a longer description with one precise word. Learn the definition carefully and notice whether the required word is a noun, adjective or another part of speech.

5. Vocabulary in context

Read the complete sentence before choosing an answer. Look for clues in nearby words, the tone of the sentence and the relationship between its ideas. A familiar synonym may still be incorrect if it does not fit the context.

6. Reading comprehension

Identify the main idea, supporting details, author's purpose and tone. Answers should be supported by the passage rather than personal assumptions.

Common mistakes
  • Choosing a word based only on a similar spelling.
  • Ignoring the context of the sentence.
  • Confusing an idiom's literal meaning with its intended meaning.
  • Choosing a one-word substitution with the wrong grammatical role.
Important formulas and quick reference
  • Abundant = plentiful.
  • Reluctant = unwilling.
  • Concise = brief but complete.
  • Optimistic = hopeful.
Worked example

Sentence: 'The instructions were concise and easy to follow.'

  1. Concise describes the instructions.
  2. The context suggests clarity without unnecessary length.
  3. Meaning: brief but complete.

CORE

Grammar: Tenses and Error Detection

Detailed concept notes
1. Understanding tense

Tense shows when an action happens. English commonly uses present, past and future time, each with simple, continuous, perfect and perfect continuous forms.

2. The twelve tense forms
  • Present simple: subject + base verb. Example: She studies.
  • Present continuous: am/is/are + verb-ing. Example: She is studying.
  • Present perfect: has/have + past participle. Example: She has studied.
  • Present perfect continuous: has/have been + verb-ing.
  • Past simple: subject + past form. Example: She studied.
  • Past continuous: was/were + verb-ing.
  • Past perfect: had + past participle.
  • Past perfect continuous: had been + verb-ing.
  • Future simple: will + base verb.
  • Future continuous: will be + verb-ing.
  • Future perfect: will have + past participle.
  • Future perfect continuous: will have been + verb-ing.
3. Subject–verb agreement

A verb must agree with its subject in number and person. A singular subject generally takes a singular verb, while a plural subject takes a plural verb.

Example: The list of items is ready. The subject is list, not items.

4. Articles

Use a before a consonant sound and an before a vowel sound. Use the when referring to a particular or already identified person or thing.

Example: an honest person, a university, the book on the table.

5. Prepositions and pronouns

Prepositions express relationships of time, place, direction and manner. Pronouns replace nouns and must agree with the nouns they refer to.

6. Common error-detection patterns
  • Incorrect subject–verb agreement.
  • Wrong tense or inconsistent tense sequence.
  • Incorrect article or preposition.
  • Pronoun disagreement or unclear reference.
  • Incorrect comparison, such as more better.
  • Unnecessary repetition or incorrect word order.
7. A reliable correction method

First identify the subject and main verb. Then check tense, agreement, articles, prepositions and pronouns. Read the corrected sentence again to ensure that its meaning remains logical.

Important formulas and quick reference
  • Present simple: subject + base verb (with third-person singular agreement).
  • Present continuous: am/is/are + verb-ing.
  • Present perfect: has/have + past participle.
  • Past perfect: had + past participle.
Worked example

Correct: 'She has completed the assignment.'

  1. Subject is she.
  2. Present perfect uses has + past participle.
  3. Completed is the past participle.

FOUNDATION

Ratio, Proportion and Time–Distance

Detailed concept notes
1. Ratio

A ratio compares two quantities by division. Write it as a:b or as the fraction a/b. Both quantities must be expressed in the same unit before comparison.

Example: 12:18 simplifies to 2:3 after dividing both terms by their highest common factor, 6.

2. Equivalent ratios and sharing quantities

Equivalent ratios are formed by multiplying or dividing both terms by the same non-zero number. To divide a total in a given ratio, add the ratio parts and divide the total by that sum.

Example: Divide 72 in the ratio 5:7. Total parts = 12. One part = 72/12 = 6. The shares are 30 and 42.

3. Proportion

Two ratios are in proportion when they are equal. If a/b = c/d, then ad = bc, provided the denominators are non-zero.

4. Direct and inverse proportion

In direct proportion, both quantities increase or decrease together at a constant rate. In inverse proportion, one quantity increases as the other decreases, while their product remains constant.

5. Speed, distance and time

Speed measures distance travelled per unit of time.

  • Speed = Distance ÷ Time
  • Distance = Speed × Time
  • Time = Distance ÷ Speed

Use consistent units. For example, distance in kilometres and time in hours give speed in kilometres per hour.

6. Unit conversion
  • 1 kilometre = 1,000 metres.
  • 1 hour = 60 minutes = 3,600 seconds.
  • To convert km/h into m/s, multiply by 5/18.
  • To convert m/s into km/h, multiply by 18/5.
7. Average speed

Average speed = total distance ÷ total time. When equal distances are travelled at speeds u and v, average speed is 2uv/(u+v). Do not simply average the two speeds unless the conditions justify it.

8. Relative speed

For two objects moving in the same direction, relative speed is the difference between their speeds. For opposite directions, add their speeds.

9. Trains and boats

For a train passing a stationary object, use the train's length as the distance. For two trains, account for both train lengths. In still water, downstream speed equals boat speed plus stream speed, while upstream speed equals boat speed minus stream speed.

Common mistakes
  • Comparing quantities expressed in different units.
  • Using the wrong time or distance unit.
  • Confusing average speed with average of speeds.
  • Adding speeds when objects move in the same direction.
Important formulas and quick reference
  • a:b = a/b.
  • a/b = c/d implies ad = bc.
  • Speed = Distance/Time.
  • Distance = Speed × Time.
  • Time = Distance/Speed.
Worked example

A vehicle travels 150 km in 3 hours.

  1. Speed = distance/time.
  2. 150/3 = 50.
  3. Average speed = 50 km/h.

CORE

Logical Reasoning: Series and Coding

Detailed concept notes
1. Number series

A number series follows a rule connecting its terms. Start by checking the difference between consecutive terms. If the differences are not constant, inspect multiplication, division, second differences, squares, cubes or alternating patterns.

2. Common series patterns
  • Constant difference: 4, 7, 10, 13 — add 3.
  • Constant multiplication: 3, 6, 12, 24 — multiply by 2.
  • Squares: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25.
  • Cubes: 1, 8, 27, 64, 125.
  • Alternating patterns: two different rules may operate in alternate positions.
3. Letter series

Convert letters into their alphabet positions: A = 1, B = 2, continuing up to Z = 26. Check whether the letters move forward or backward by a fixed or changing number of positions.

4. Coding and decoding

Coding questions change letters, numbers or words according to a hidden rule. Compare the original and coded forms to identify the transformation.

  • Letter shifting: moving each letter a fixed number of places.
  • Reverse alphabet: matching A with Z, B with Y and so on.
  • Position coding: replacing letters with their alphabet numbers.
  • Rearrangement: changing the order of letters or symbols.
5. Word and number relationships

Some questions use a relationship between words, letters or numbers. Identify the operation or rule using all the examples before applying it to the unknown term.

6. A step-by-step solving method
  1. Write down the differences between consecutive terms.
  2. Check multiplication or division if differences do not reveal a pattern.
  3. Inspect alternate terms separately.
  4. For letter series, convert letters to positions.
  5. Test the proposed rule against every given term.
Common mistakes
  • Assuming a pattern after checking only two terms.
  • Ignoring alternating sequences.
  • Making alphabet shifts without accounting for the end of the alphabet.
  • Spending too long on one difficult pattern instead of moving on and returning later.
Important formulas and quick reference
  • Alphabet positions: A = 1 through Z = 26.
  • Square numbers: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25...
  • Cube numbers: 1, 8, 27, 64...
  • Constant difference: a, a+d, a+2d...
Worked example

Find the next term: 3, 6, 12, 24.

  1. Compare consecutive terms.
  2. Each term is multiplied by 2.
  3. Next term = 24 × 2 = 48.

Chapter 1: Indian History and the National Movement

1. Understanding Indian History

Indian history is commonly studied through three broad periods: Ancient, Medieval and Modern India. Each period helps explain how Indian society, administration, culture and political institutions developed.

Ancient India

  • Indus Valley Civilisation: Known for planned cities, drainage systems, standardised weights, trade and craft production. Harappa and Mohenjo-daro are important sites.
  • Vedic Period: The Vedas are major sources for understanding early Vedic society, religious practices and ideas.
  • Mauryan Empire: Chandragupta Maurya established the empire. Ashoka is remembered for his inscriptions and promotion of Dhamma after the Kalinga War.
  • Gupta Period: Often associated with developments in mathematics, astronomy, literature, art and learning.

Medieval India

Important areas include the Delhi Sultanate, the Mughal Empire, regional kingdoms, the Bhakti movement and the Sufi tradition. Study major rulers, administrative systems, architecture and cultural developments.

Modern India

  • European arrival: European trading companies established commercial settlements before British political control expanded.
  • Revolt of 1857: A major uprising against East India Company rule, involving soldiers, rulers and civilians in different regions.
  • Indian National Congress: Founded in 1885; it became a major platform in the national movement.
  • Partition of Bengal: Announced in 1905 and followed by the Swadeshi movement.
  • Non-Cooperation Movement: Launched in 1920 under Mahatma Gandhi.
  • Civil Disobedience Movement: Associated with the Salt March of 1930.
  • Quit India Movement: Launched in 1942 with the demand for an end to British rule.
  • Independence: India became independent on 15 August 1947.

How to study historical events

Connect each event with its year, leading personalities, location, immediate cause and result. A timeline is more useful than memorising disconnected dates.

Worked example

Question: Which movement is directly associated with the Dandi March of 1930?

Solution: The Dandi March challenged the British salt laws and marked the beginning of the Civil Disobedience Movement.

Practice questions

  1. In which year was the Indian National Congress founded?
  2. Which Mauryan ruler is associated with the Kalinga War?
  3. Which movement began with the Dandi March?
Reveal answers

1. 1885. 2. Ashoka. 3. Civil Disobedience Movement.

Chapter 2: Indian and World Geography

1. What is Geography?

Geography studies Earth's physical features, climate, natural resources, population and human activities. Physical geography focuses on natural systems, while human geography studies people and their relationship with places.

Physical Geography

  • Latitude: Angular distance north or south of the Equator. The Equator is at 0° latitude.
  • Longitude: Angular distance east or west of the Prime Meridian. It helps determine time zones.
  • Rotation: Earth's rotation on its axis causes day and night.
  • Revolution: Earth's movement around the Sun, together with its axial tilt, produces seasonal changes.
  • Atmosphere: The gaseous envelope surrounding Earth. Its main layers include the troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere and exosphere.
  • Weather: Short-term atmospheric conditions. Climate: Long-term average weather patterns.

Indian Geography

  • Himalayas: Young fold mountains in northern India.
  • Northern Plains: Extensive fertile plains formed largely by river deposits.
  • Peninsular Plateau: An ancient region containing several mineral resources.
  • Thar Desert: Located mainly in Rajasthan.
  • Coastal Plains: Western and eastern coastal regions with different physical characteristics.
  • Islands: Andaman and Nicobar Islands lie in the Bay of Bengal; Lakshadweep lies in the Arabian Sea.

Indian Rivers and Monsoon

The Ganga, Brahmaputra and Indus systems are major Himalayan river systems. The Narmada and Tapi flow westwards. The southwest monsoon supplies a major share of India's annual rainfall.

Worked example

Question: Why do places at different longitudes experience different local solar times?

Solution: Earth rotates from west to east. Different longitudes therefore face the Sun at different moments. A 15° difference in longitude corresponds approximately to one hour of solar time.

Practice questions

  1. What is the latitude of the Equator?
  2. Which Indian river flows westwards between the Vindhya and Satpura ranges?
  3. Which island group lies in the Arabian Sea?
Reveal answers

1. 0°. 2. Narmada. 3. Lakshadweep.

Chapter 3: Indian Polity and Constitution

1. The Constitution of India

The Constitution is the supreme legal framework of India. It defines the structure of government, distributes powers, establishes institutions and protects citizens' rights.

  • Adopted: 26 November 1949.
  • Enforced: 26 January 1950.
  • Preamble: Describes India as a sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic republic and expresses the objectives of justice, liberty, equality and fraternity.
  • Fundamental Rights: Constitutional protections available under Part III.
  • Directive Principles: Guidelines for the State in Part IV.
  • Fundamental Duties: Duties of citizens listed under Article 51A.

Three organs of government

  • Legislature: Makes laws. At the Union level, Parliament consists of the President and two Houses.
  • Executive: Implements laws and administers government. The President is the constitutional head of the Union.
  • Judiciary: Interprets laws and protects constitutional principles. The Supreme Court is India's highest court.

Important constitutional ideas

Federalism distributes powers between the Union and States. Separation of powers divides major government functions among different institutions. Judicial review allows courts to examine whether laws and government actions comply with the Constitution.

Parliamentary system

The Council of Ministers, headed by the Prime Minister, is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. The Rajya Sabha is the Council of States and is a permanent House.

Worked example

Question: Which part of the Constitution contains Fundamental Rights?

Solution: Part III contains Fundamental Rights. Part IV contains Directive Principles of State Policy.

Practice questions

  1. On which date did the Constitution come into force?
  2. Which Article is associated with Fundamental Duties?
  3. Which is the highest court in India?
Reveal answers

1. 26 January 1950. 2. Article 51A. 3. Supreme Court of India.

Chapter 4: Economy, Science and Environment

Basic Economics

  • GDP: The monetary value of final goods and services produced within a country's domestic territory during a specified period.
  • Inflation: A sustained rise in the general price level, reducing the purchasing power of money.
  • Repo rate: The rate at which the Reserve Bank of India lends short-term funds to banks against eligible securities.
  • Fiscal policy: Government decisions about taxation, public expenditure and borrowing.
  • Monetary policy: Central bank measures that influence money, credit and interest-rate conditions.
  • Budget: The government's annual statement of estimated receipts and expenditure.

Basic Science

  • Physics: Study motion, force, energy, heat, light, sound, electricity and magnetism.
  • Chemistry: Study matter, atoms, elements, compounds, chemical reactions, acids, bases and salts.
  • Biology: Study living organisms, cells, tissues, organ systems, genetics and ecosystems.
  • Units: Force is measured in newtons, energy in joules, power in watts and electric current in amperes.

Environment and Ecology

An ecosystem includes living organisms and their physical environment. Producers make their own food, consumers obtain energy by feeding on other organisms, and decomposers break down dead organic matter.

  • Food chain: A sequence showing the transfer of energy between organisms.
  • Biodiversity: Variety of life at genetic, species and ecosystem levels.
  • Greenhouse effect: Warming caused when atmospheric gases absorb and re-emit outgoing infrared radiation.
  • Conservation: Protection and sustainable management of natural resources.

Organisations and current awareness

Prepare the purpose and headquarters of major national and international organisations, important awards, sports events, books, personalities, scientific developments and significant national and international events. Current-affairs notes should be dated and updated regularly rather than treated as permanent facts.

Defence awareness

Study the broad roles of the Indian Army, Navy and Air Force; important national defence institutions; major defence-related appointments; and significant developments reported in reliable official announcements. Focus on general knowledge and institutional roles.

Worked example

Question: Which institution is responsible for India's monetary policy?

Solution: The Reserve Bank of India is India's central bank and is responsible for monetary policy.

Practice questions

  1. What does GDP measure?
  2. Which unit is used to measure power?
  3. What is the role of decomposers in an ecosystem?
Reveal answers

1. The value of final goods and services produced domestically during a period. 2. Watt. 3. They break down dead organic matter and recycle nutrients.

Additional Numerical Ability Topics Number systems, arithmetic, interest, mensuration, statistics and probability ⌄
Chapter 1: Number System, Fractions, Decimals, HCF and LCM

Types of numbers

  • Natural numbers: Counting numbers 1, 2, 3, ...
  • Whole numbers: 0 and all natural numbers.
  • Integers: Negative whole numbers, zero and positive whole numbers.
  • Rational numbers: Numbers expressible as a fraction of two integers with a non-zero denominator.
  • Irrational numbers: Numbers that cannot be expressed as such a fraction, for example √2.

Fractions and decimals

A fraction represents a part of a whole. To add or subtract fractions, first use a common denominator. To multiply fractions, multiply numerators and denominators. To divide, multiply by the reciprocal of the second fraction.

To convert a fraction into a decimal, divide its numerator by its denominator. To convert a terminating decimal into a fraction, write it over 10, 100, 1000 and simplify.

HCF and LCM

HCF is the greatest positive integer that divides each given integer exactly. LCM is the smallest positive integer that is a multiple of every given integer.

For two positive integers:

HCF × LCM = Product of the two numbers.

Powers and roots

For a non-zero number a, \(a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n}\), and \(a^m \div a^n = a^{m-n}\) when the division is defined. A square root of a non-negative number is a value whose square equals that number.

Worked examples

Example 1: Find the HCF and LCM of 12 and 18.

Factors common to both include 1, 2, 3 and 6. Therefore HCF = 6. The smallest common multiple is 36, so LCM = 36.

Example 2: Convert 0.375 into a fraction.

\(0.375 = \frac{375}{1000} = \frac{3}{8}\).

Practice questions

  1. Find the HCF of 24 and 36.
  2. Find the LCM of 8 and 12.
  3. Express 0.625 as a fraction in simplest form.
Reveal answers

1. 12. 2. 24. 3. 5/8.

Chapter 2: Averages, Percentages and Interest

Average

The arithmetic mean is the sum of all observations divided by the number of observations.

\(\text{Mean}=\frac{\text{Sum of observations}}{\text{Number of observations}}\)

If the average of n values is A, their total is nA. This relationship is useful when one value is added, removed or replaced.

Percentage

Percentage means per hundred. To convert a fraction into a percentage, multiply by 100.

\(\text{Percentage}=\frac{\text{Part}}{\text{Whole}}\times100\)

For successive percentage changes, multiply the corresponding factors. An increase of x% followed by a decrease of y% gives the final factor \((1+\frac{x}{100})(1-\frac{y}{100})\).

Simple Interest

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal.

\(SI=\frac{P\times R\times T}{100}\)

Here P is principal, R is annual interest rate in percent and T is time in years. Amount = P + SI.

Compound Interest

Compound interest is calculated on the principal plus previously accumulated interest.

\(A=P(1+\frac{R}{100})^T\)

Compound interest = A − P, for annual compounding. For other compounding intervals, the rate and number of periods must be adjusted.

Worked examples

Example 1: Find the average of 12, 18, 20 and 10.

Sum = 60 and number of values = 4. Average = 60 ÷ 4 = 15.

Example 2: Find simple interest on ₹2,000 at 5% per year for 2 years.

\(SI=\frac{2000\times5\times2}{100}=₹200\).

Practice questions

  1. Find the average of 15, 25, 30 and 10.
  2. What is 18% of 500?
  3. Find simple interest on ₹3,000 at 4% per year for 3 years.
Reveal answers

1. 20. 2. 90. 3. ₹360.

Chapter 3: Time and Work, Pipes and Cisterns

Work and efficiency

If a person completes a job in n days, the person's one-day work is 1/n of the job. If two people work together, add their individual one-day work rates.

\(\text{Combined rate}=\frac{1}{x}+\frac{1}{y}\)

For two workers who take x and y days individually, their combined time is \(\frac{xy}{x+y}\), provided their work rates remain constant.

Efficiency

Efficiency measures the amount of work completed per unit of time. For the same job, time is inversely proportional to efficiency. A worker who is twice as efficient takes half as much time.

Pipes and cisterns

An inlet fills a tank and an outlet empties it. Treat filling as a positive rate and emptying as a negative rate. Add the rates to find the net rate.

Worked examples

Example 1: A completes a job in 6 days and B in 3 days. How long do they take together?

Combined rate = 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/2 of the job per day. Time = 2 days.

Example 2: An inlet fills a tank in 4 hours and an outlet empties it in 12 hours. How long will the tank take to fill if both are open?

Net rate = 1/4 − 1/12 = 1/6 tank per hour. Time = 6 hours.

Practice questions

  1. A completes a job in 8 days and B in 8 days. Find their combined time.
  2. A pipe fills a tank in 5 hours. What fraction does it fill in one hour?
  3. An inlet fills a tank in 6 hours and an outlet empties it in 12 hours. Find the net filling time.
Reveal answers

1. 4 days. 2. 1/5 of the tank. 3. 12 hours.

Chapter 4: Mixtures, Alligation and Profit Applications

Mixtures

A mixture combines two or more substances. Questions may ask for the resulting concentration, average price or quantity of each component.

For two ingredients, total quantity is the sum of their quantities. The amount of a component in a mixture equals its fraction multiplied by the total quantity.

Alligation

Alligation is a shortcut for finding the ratio in which two items with different values or concentrations must be mixed to obtain a required mean value.

If the lower value is L, the higher value is H and the desired mean is M, then:

Quantity of higher-value item : Quantity of lower-value item = (M − L) : (H − M)

This assumes L is less than M and M is less than H.

Profit, loss and discount

Profit = Selling Price − Cost Price. Loss = Cost Price − Selling Price.

\(\text{Profit \%}=\frac{\text{Profit}}{\text{Cost Price}}\times100\)

\(\text{Discount}=\text{Marked Price}-\text{Selling Price}\)

Profit or loss percentage is normally calculated on cost price, while discount percentage is calculated on marked price.

Worked examples

Example 1: In what ratio should items costing ₹20 and ₹40 be mixed to obtain a mixture worth ₹30?

Higher : Lower = (30 − 20) : (40 − 30) = 10 : 10 = 1 : 1.

Example 2: An item costing ₹400 is sold for ₹460. Find the profit percentage.

Profit = ₹60. Profit percentage = 60/400 × 100 = 15%.

Practice questions

  1. Find the profit percentage when cost price is ₹500 and selling price is ₹575.
  2. An item marked at ₹800 is sold at a 10% discount. Find its selling price.
  3. Find the ratio for mixing values 10 and 30 to obtain a mean value of 20.
Reveal answers

1. 15%. 2. ₹720. 3. 1 : 1.

Chapter 5: Mensuration, Area and Perimeter

Basic terms

Perimeter is the total distance around a two-dimensional shape. Area measures the surface enclosed by the shape. Volume measures the space occupied by a three-dimensional object.

Important two-dimensional formulas

  • Square: Area = side²; perimeter = 4 × side.
  • Rectangle: Area = length × breadth; perimeter = 2(length + breadth).
  • Triangle: Area = ½ × base × height.
  • Circle: Area = πr²; circumference = 2πr.
  • Parallelogram: Area = base × perpendicular height.
  • Trapezium: Area = ½ × (sum of parallel sides) × height.

Important three-dimensional formulas

  • Cube: Volume = a³; total surface area = 6a².
  • Cuboid: Volume = lbh; total surface area = 2(lb + bh + hl).
  • Cylinder: Volume = πr²h; curved surface area = 2πrh.
  • Cone: Volume = ⅓πr²h.
  • Sphere: Volume = ⁴⁄₃πr³; surface area = 4πr².

Use consistent units. If dimensions are in centimetres, area is in square centimetres and volume is in cubic centimetres.

Worked example

Question: Find the area and perimeter of a rectangle with length 12 cm and breadth 5 cm.

Area = 12 × 5 = 60 cm². Perimeter = 2(12 + 5) = 34 cm.

Practice questions

  1. Find the area of a square with side 9 cm.
  2. Find the circumference of a circle of radius 7 cm, using π = 22/7.
  3. Find the volume of a cube with side 4 cm.
Reveal answers

1. 81 cm². 2. 44 cm. 3. 64 cm³.

Chapter 6: Statistics, Probability and Heights and Distances

Statistics

  • Mean: Sum of observations divided by their count.
  • Median: Middle value after arranging observations in order. For an even number of observations, it is the average of the two middle values.
  • Mode: The value that occurs most frequently.
  • Range: Highest value minus lowest value.

Probability

Probability measures the likelihood of an event. For equally likely outcomes:

\(P(E)=\frac{\text{Favourable outcomes}}{\text{Total possible outcomes}}\)

Probability lies between 0 and 1. An impossible event has probability 0, while a certain event has probability 1.

For complementary events, \(P(\text{not }E)=1-P(E)\).

Heights and distances

These questions use right-angled triangles and trigonometric ratios. The angle of elevation is measured upwards from the horizontal; the angle of depression is measured downwards from the horizontal.

  • \(\sin\theta=\frac{\text{Perpendicular}}{\text{Hypotenuse}}\)
  • \(\cos\theta=\frac{\text{Base}}{\text{Hypotenuse}}\)
  • \(\tan\theta=\frac{\text{Perpendicular}}{\text{Base}}\)

Choose the ratio that connects the known side with the unknown side. Draw a labelled triangle before calculating.

Worked examples

Example 1: Find the median of 3, 8, 5, 2 and 7.

Arrange the values: 2, 3, 5, 7, 8. The middle value is 5.

Example 2: A fair die is rolled. What is the probability of obtaining an even number?

Favourable outcomes = 2, 4, 6 (three outcomes). Total outcomes = 6. Probability = 3/6 = 1/2.

Practice questions

  1. Find the mode of 2, 3, 3, 4, 5.
  2. What is the probability of getting a number greater than 4 on a fair die?
  3. Which trigonometric ratio connects the perpendicular and base of a right triangle?
Reveal answers

1. 3. 2. 2/6 = 1/3. 3. Tangent.

Chapter 1: Verbal Reasoning and Logical Relationships

Analogy

An analogy tests whether you can identify a relationship between two items and apply the same relationship to another pair.

Example: Bird : Nest :: Bee : Hive. A nest is a bird's home, just as a hive is a bee's home.

Classification and odd one out

Identify the common property shared by most options. The remaining option is the odd one out. Compare categories, uses, shapes, meanings or numerical properties.

Number and letter series

Look for repeated operations, differences, multiplication, alternating patterns or changes in alphabet positions.

For alphabet questions, use A = 1, B = 2, ... Z = 26. Remember that sequences may restart after Z.

Coding and decoding

Find the rule used to change letters or numbers. Common patterns include fixed forward or backward shifts, reversed order and position-based changes.

Blood relations and directions

For family relationships, draw a small family tree and mark each generation. For direction questions, establish a fixed north-south-east-west reference before following turns.

Worked examples

Example 1: Complete the series: 3, 6, 12, 24, ?

Each term is multiplied by 2. The next term is 48.

Example 2: If CAT is coded as DBU by shifting every letter one place forward, how is DOG coded?

D becomes E, O becomes P and G becomes H. Answer: EPH.

Practice questions

  1. Complete: 5, 10, 20, 40, ?
  2. Find the odd one out: Triangle, Square, Circle, Rectangle.
  3. If each letter is shifted one place forward, code SUN.
Reveal answers

1. 80. 2. Circle, because it has no straight sides. 3. TVO.

Chapter 2: Non-Verbal Reasoning and Figure Patterns

What is non-verbal reasoning?

Non-verbal reasoning tests the ability to understand information through shapes, diagrams, symbols and visual sequences rather than written statements.

Common question types

  • Figure series: Identify how a shape changes from one frame to the next.
  • Figure analogy: Apply the transformation seen in one pair of figures to another pair.
  • Figure classification: Find the shape that does not follow the common rule.
  • Mirror images: Understand how a figure appears after reflection across a mirror line.
  • Water images: Understand reflection across a horizontal surface.
  • Embedded figures: Locate a smaller shape hidden within a more complex figure.
  • Paper folding: Track how folds and openings affect the final pattern.

How to solve figure series

  1. Check whether the number of shapes changes.
  2. Observe the direction of movement or rotation.
  3. Compare shading, size, position and orientation.
  4. Look for alternating changes between odd and even positions.
  5. Test the rule against every figure before choosing an answer.

Rotation and reflection

A rotation turns a figure around a fixed point. A reflection creates a reversed image across a line. A 90-degree rotation is different from a mirror reflection, so check the orientation of asymmetric details such as arrows or letters.

Worked example

Question: An arrow points upward. It is rotated clockwise by 90 degrees. Which direction does it point?

Solution: A clockwise quarter-turn changes upward to right.

Practice questions

  1. An arrow points left. What direction will it point after a 180-degree rotation?
  2. Which transformation reverses left and right across a vertical line?
  3. In a figure series, a shape rotates 90 degrees clockwise at each step. What is its orientation after four steps?
Reveal answers

1. Right. 2. Reflection. 3. Its original orientation.

Chapter 3: Spatial Orientation, Directions and Position

Spatial ability

Spatial reasoning is the ability to understand the position, direction and movement of objects in space. It includes mentally rotating shapes, following routes and identifying relative positions.

Direction basics

The four main directions are North, South, East and West. The intermediate directions are North-East, North-West, South-East and South-West.

  • Facing north: right is east and left is west.
  • Facing south: right is west and left is east.
  • Facing east: right is south and left is north.
  • Facing west: right is north and left is south.

Solving route questions

Draw a simple coordinate-style path. Mark the starting point, every turn and each distance. Opposite movements cancel when calculating final displacement.

Position and arrangement

Questions may describe objects as above, below, left, right, between, nearest or farthest. Convert each statement into a small diagram rather than trying to remember the entire description.

Worked example

Question: A person faces north, turns right, then turns right again. Which direction is the person facing?

Solution: North → East → South. The final direction is south.

Practice questions

  1. A person faces east and turns left. Which direction are they facing?
  2. What is the opposite direction of south-west?
  3. A person walks 5 km north and then 5 km south. What is the final displacement?
Reveal answers

1. North. 2. North-East. 3. Zero; the person returns to the starting point.

Chapter 4: Figure Counting, Cubes and Visualisation

Figure counting

These questions ask you to count shapes such as triangles, squares or rectangles hidden inside a larger diagram. Count systematically from the smallest shapes to larger combinations.

Counting triangles

First count the smallest individual triangles. Then look for combinations formed by joining two or more smaller regions. Check different sizes and orientations separately to avoid double counting.

Counting squares and rectangles

For a grid, count individual cells first, then larger shapes made by combining cells. A rectangle must have four right angles; a square also has four equal sides.

Cube visualisation

Cube questions may test opposite faces, adjacent faces and how a cube appears after rotation. Keep track of which faces share an edge. Opposite faces cannot be adjacent.

Paper folding and cutting

Imagine each fold as a reflection. When a folded paper is opened, a cut or hole is repeated symmetrically across each fold line.

Worked example

Question: A square is divided into four equal smaller squares. How many smallest squares are present?

Solution: There are four individual small squares. The entire outer square is an additional larger square, making five squares in total.

Practice questions

  1. How many faces does a cube have?
  2. How many edges does a cube have?
  3. How many vertices does a cube have?
Reveal answers

1. Six. 2. Twelve. 3. Eight.
