Subject–Verb Agreement

Detailed concept notes
1. Understanding subject–verb agreement

Subject–verb agreement means that the verb must match its subject in number and person. A singular subject generally takes a singular verb, while a plural subject generally takes a plural verb.

Examples: "The student works hard." "The students work hard."

Do not choose a verb based only on the noun nearest to it. First identify the actual subject of the sentence.

2. Agreement with singular and plural subjects
  • A singular noun generally takes a singular verb: The child plays.
  • A plural noun generally takes a plural verb: The children play.
  • Two subjects joined by "and" generally take a plural verb: Riya and Aman are present.
  • When two words refer to one combined idea or one unit, a singular verb may be appropriate: Bread and butter is my usual breakfast.
3. Subjects separated from their verbs

Words and phrases placed between a subject and its verb do not normally change the number of the subject.

Example: The collection of old coins is valuable.

The subject is "collection", not "coins". Therefore, the singular verb "is" is correct.

Common interrupting phrases include "along with", "as well as", "together with", "in addition to" and "including".

Example: The captain, along with the players, is ready.

4. Indefinite pronouns

Some indefinite pronouns are normally singular, some are plural, and some depend on the noun they refer to.

Usually singular: Each, either, neither, everyone, everybody, someone, somebody, anyone, anybody, no one and nobody.

Example: Everyone is invited.

Usually plural: Both, few, many and several.

Example: Several have completed the assignment.

Depends on context: All, some, most, none and any. Their verb agreement depends on whether they refer to a singular or plural countable noun, or an uncountable noun.

Examples: Some of the water is missing. Some of the books are missing.

5. Either/or and neither/nor

When subjects are joined by "either...or" or "neither...nor", the verb generally agrees with the subject nearest to it.

Examples:

  • Neither the teacher nor the students are available.
  • Neither the students nor the teacher is available.
  • Either the manager or the assistants are attending.

When the sentence sounds awkward because the subjects have different numbers, rewriting it can improve clarity.

6. Collective nouns

A collective noun describes a group as one unit. Examples include team, committee, family, audience, jury and government.

When the group is treated as one unit, a singular verb is commonly used: The committee has reached a decision.

When the members are emphasised as individuals, plural agreement may be used in varieties of English that allow it: The committee are divided in their opinions.

For formal examination questions, follow the intended meaning and the variety of English used in the question.

7. Subjects that look plural but are singular

Some nouns end in "s" but are treated as singular when they name a subject, field or activity.

  • Mathematics is an important subject.
  • Physics is studied in this course.
  • The news is encouraging.
  • Measles is an infectious disease.

Some nouns are plural in meaning and normally take plural verbs, such as "police" and "cattle".

Example: The police are investigating the case.

8. Amounts, distances and periods as a unit

A single amount, measurement, distance or period considered as one whole generally takes a singular verb.

  • Ten kilometres is a long distance to walk.
  • Five hundred rupees is enough for the purchase.
  • Three hours is sufficient for the examination.

If the sentence focuses on separate individual items or units, the meaning may require a plural verb.

9. Agreement with "there" and "here"

In sentences beginning with "there is", "there are", "here is" or "here are", the verb agrees with the actual subject that follows it.

  • There is a book on the table.
  • There are three books on the table.
  • Here is the document you requested.
  • Here are the documents you requested.
10. Relative pronouns and agreement

Words such as who, which and that introduce relative clauses. The verb in the relative clause agrees with the noun or pronoun to which the relative pronoun refers.

Example: She is one of the students who work regularly.

Here, "who" refers to "students", so the plural verb "work" is used.

Compare: She is the only one of the students who works regularly. Here, "who" refers to "the only one".

11. Agreement with titles and names

The title of a book, film, organisation or work is generally treated as singular even when it contains plural-looking words.

Example: "The Arabian Nights" is a famous collection of stories.

The title is considered one work.

12. Common examination errors
  • Choosing the verb based on a nearby noun instead of the true subject.
  • Using a plural verb after "each" or "everyone".
  • Forgetting that "along with" does not normally create a compound subject.
  • Using a singular verb with a clearly plural subject joined by "and".
  • Ignoring the nearest-subject rule in "either/or" and "neither/nor" constructions.
  • Confusing collective nouns with ordinary plural nouns.
13. A reliable sentence-correction method
  1. Locate the main verb.
  2. Ask who or what performs the action or is described by that verb.
  3. Identify the complete subject and ignore interrupting phrases.
  4. Determine whether the subject is singular or plural.
  5. Check special constructions such as each, either/or, neither/nor and collective nouns.
  6. Read the sentence again to ensure that it is grammatically and logically clear.
14. Advanced practice approach

In difficult questions, examine the sentence structure rather than relying on sound. A long phrase between the subject and verb can hide the real subject. Pay particular attention to relative clauses, indefinite pronouns, collective nouns and compound subjects.

Important formulas and quick reference
  • He works; they work.
  • Each of the students is ready.
  • The list of items is on the desk.
  • Neither the teacher nor the students are absent.
Worked example

Choose the correct form: 'The quality of these products ___ excellent.'

  1. Find the subject: quality.
  2. 'Of these products' is a modifying phrase.
  3. Quality is singular, so use is.

Vocabulary and Reading Comprehension

Detailed concept notes
1. Building a strong vocabulary

Vocabulary is the collection of words a person understands and uses. A strong vocabulary improves comprehension, sentence completion, precise expression and the ability to identify the intended meaning of unfamiliar words.

Learning a word should involve more than memorising one definition. Study its pronunciation, meaning, part of speech, common usage, word family, synonyms and antonyms.

2. Synonyms

Synonyms are words with similar or closely related meanings. They are not always interchangeable because context, tone and intensity may differ.

  • Abundant: plentiful, ample, copious.
  • Reluctant: unwilling, hesitant, disinclined.
  • Rapid: swift, quick, speedy.
  • Accurate: correct, precise, exact.
  • Ancient: old, historic, age-old.
  • Brief: short, concise, compact.

Exam tip: Replace the target word with each option mentally and check whether the sentence still conveys the same meaning.

3. Antonyms

Antonyms are words with opposite or contrasting meanings. Some opposites are direct, while others depend on context.

  • Expand — contract.
  • Reluctant — willing.
  • Generous — stingy.
  • Optimistic — pessimistic.
  • Transparent — opaque.
  • Permanent — temporary.
  • Victory — defeat.
  • Artificial — natural.

Do not choose an option merely because it looks different from the given word. Check that it expresses the relevant opposite.

4. Contextual vocabulary

A word can have different meanings in different situations. Contextual vocabulary questions test whether you can identify the meaning intended in a particular sentence or passage.

Consider the word "bright": it can describe strong light, a vivid colour, an intelligent person or a promising future.

Solving method:

  1. Read the complete sentence.
  2. Identify the subject and situation.
  3. Look for nearby clues, examples, contrasts or explanations.
  4. Replace the word with a possible meaning.
  5. Choose the option that preserves the sentence's intended meaning.
5. Roots, prefixes and suffixes

Many English words are formed from smaller meaningful parts. Recognising these parts can help you estimate the meaning of unfamiliar vocabulary.

Common prefixes:

  • un-: not or opposite, as in unhappy.
  • re-: again, as in rewrite.
  • pre-: before, as in preview.
  • mis-: wrongly, as in misunderstand.
  • anti-: against, as in antifreeze.
  • sub-: under or below, as in submarine.
  • inter-: between, as in international.

Common suffixes:

  • -able: capable of, as in readable.
  • -less: without, as in careless.
  • -ful: full of, as in hopeful.
  • -tion: action or process, as in education.
  • -ist: a person associated with an activity or field, as in scientist.
  • -ology: study of, as in biology.

Word parts are clues, not guarantees. Always verify the meaning through context.

6. Commonly useful word families
  • Decide — decision — decisive — indecisive.
  • Compete — competition — competitive — competitor.
  • Observe — observation — observant — observer.
  • Permit — permission — permissible.
  • Analyse — analysis — analytical — analyst.
  • Success — succeed — successful — successfully.

Recognising word families helps identify the correct form of a word in sentence-completion questions.

7. One-word substitution

One-word substitution questions ask you to replace a phrase with one precise word.

  • A person who speaks many languages: polyglot.
  • A person who loves books: bibliophile.
  • A person who cannot read or write: illiterate.
  • A person who studies plants: botanist.
  • A person who studies insects: entomologist.
  • A speech delivered without preparation: impromptu.
  • A place where birds are kept: aviary.
  • A life story written by oneself: autobiography.
  • A person who looks at the bright side: optimist.
  • A person who expects the worst: pessimist.

Learn the exact distinction between similar terms. For example, a biography is written about a person, while an autobiography is written by that person.

8. Idioms and phrases

An idiom is an expression whose overall meaning is different from the literal meanings of its individual words.

  • Break the ice: make people feel comfortable at the beginning of an interaction.
  • Once in a blue moon: very rarely.
  • Hit the nail on the head: identify the exact point.
  • Under the weather: feeling unwell.
  • Spill the beans: reveal a secret.
  • A blessing in disguise: something that appears unfortunate but leads to a good result.
  • At the eleventh hour: at the last possible moment.
  • Burn the midnight oil: work or study late into the night.
  • Call it a day: stop working for the present.
  • In hot water: in trouble.

Understand the meaning and practise using each idiom in a complete sentence.

9. Reading comprehension: purpose and structure

Reading comprehension tests whether you can understand a passage, identify its main idea, locate supporting details, infer unstated information and recognise the author's attitude.

Questions may ask about the central theme, a specific fact, the meaning of a word, the author's purpose, a suitable title or an inference.

10. Main idea and supporting details

The main idea is the central message of the passage. Supporting details provide reasons, examples, facts or explanations that develop it.

A useful distinction is:

  • Topic: What the passage is about.
  • Main idea: What the author says about that topic.
  • Supporting detail: Evidence or explanation that develops the main idea.

Do not select an answer that describes only one small example when the question asks for the central idea.

11. Inference and conclusion

An inference is a logical conclusion drawn from information in the passage, even when it is not directly stated.

For example, if a passage says that dark clouds gathered, the wind became stronger and people opened their umbrellas, it may be reasonable to infer that rain was expected or had begun.

An inference must be supported by the passage. Do not introduce outside assumptions or select an option that goes beyond the available evidence.

12. Author's tone and purpose

The tone is the author's attitude towards the subject. Common tones include:

  • Optimistic: hopeful about the future.
  • Pessimistic: expects an unfavourable outcome.
  • Critical: identifies weaknesses or problems.
  • Analytical: examines an issue logically.
  • Persuasive: attempts to influence the reader.
  • Neutral: presents information without strong emotional judgement.
  • Satirical: uses humour or irony to criticise.

The author's purpose may be to inform, explain, entertain, compare, criticise or persuade.

13. Sentence completion and contextual clues

Sentence completion tests vocabulary, grammar and logical relationships. Look for clues such as contrast, cause, result, examples and comparisons.

  • Contrast clues: although, however, despite, whereas.
  • Cause and result: because, therefore, consequently, thus.
  • Examples: for instance, such as, namely.
  • Addition: furthermore, moreover, also.

Read the complete sentence after inserting the option. The result should be grammatically correct and logically consistent.

14. A reliable passage-solving strategy
  1. Read the passage to understand its overall subject and argument.
  2. Identify the central idea and paragraph structure.
  3. Read each question carefully and identify what it asks.
  4. Return to the relevant portion of the passage for evidence.
  5. Eliminate options that contradict the passage or introduce unsupported information.
  6. Choose the answer that is best supported, not merely the one that sounds familiar.
15. Common comprehension traps
  • Confusing an inference with a directly stated fact.
  • Choosing an option that is true in general but unsupported by the passage.
  • Ignoring words such as "not", "except", "mainly" or "best".
  • Selecting an extreme statement when the passage makes a limited claim.
  • Using personal opinions instead of the author's evidence.
  • Choosing a synonym without considering the sentence context.
16. Vocabulary revision method

Maintain a vocabulary notebook with the word, meaning, part of speech, one synonym, one antonym and an example sentence. Review words periodically and practise identifying their meanings in unfamiliar sentences. Learning words in context is generally more useful than memorising isolated lists.

Important formulas and quick reference
  • Synonym: similar meaning.
  • Antonym: opposite meaning.
  • Inference: conclusion supported by evidence.
  • Main idea: central message of a passage.
Worked example

Passage: 'Regular exercise improves stamina and supports general well-being. Consistency is more useful than occasional intense activity.'

  1. Identify the repeated idea: regularity.
  2. Find the contrast: consistency versus occasional intensity.
  3. Main idea: sustainable habits support well-being.

Percentages, Profit and Loss

Detailed concept notes
1. Understanding percentage-based arithmetic

Percentages express a quantity as a fraction of 100. In commercial arithmetic, they are used to calculate profit, loss, discount, tax and changes in price.

p% = p/100.

Example: 10% of ₹450 = (10/100) × 450 = ₹45.

2. Cost price and selling price

Cost Price (CP): The amount paid to acquire or produce an item.

Selling Price (SP): The amount received when the item is sold.

  • If SP is greater than CP, there is a profit.
  • If SP is less than CP, there is a loss.
  • If SP equals CP, there is neither profit nor loss.

Profit = SP − CP.

Loss = CP − SP.

3. Calculating profit percentage

Profit percentage is calculated on cost price, not selling price.

Profit% = (Profit / CP) × 100.

Worked example: An item costs ₹200 and is sold for ₹240.

  1. Profit = 240 − 200 = ₹40.
  2. Profit percentage = (40/200) × 100.
  3. Profit percentage = 20%.
4. Calculating loss percentage

Loss percentage is also calculated on cost price.

Loss% = (Loss / CP) × 100.

Worked example: An item costing ₹750 is sold at a loss of 20%.

  1. Loss = 20% of 750 = ₹150.
  2. Selling price = 750 − 150.
  3. Therefore SP = ₹600.
5. Finding selling price from a profit or loss rate

When the profit rate is p%, the selling price is:

SP = CP × (100 + p)/100.

When the loss rate is l%, the selling price is:

SP = CP × (100 − l)/100.

Example: Find the selling price of an item costing ₹800 when the profit is 15%.

SP = 800 × 115/100 = ₹920.

6. Finding cost price from selling price

If an item is sold at a profit of p%, then:

CP = SP × 100/(100 + p).

If it is sold at a loss of l%, then:

CP = SP × 100/(100 − l).

Example: An item sells for ₹600 at a loss of 20%.

CP = 600 × 100/80 = ₹750.

7. Marked price and discount

Marked Price (MP): The displayed or listed price before discount.

Discount: The reduction from the marked price.

Discount = MP − SP.

Discount% = (Discount / MP) × 100.

Worked example: An item marked at ₹800 receives a 15% discount.

  1. Discount = 15% of 800 = ₹120.
  2. Selling price = 800 − 120 = ₹680.
8. Successive discounts

When two discounts are applied one after another, the second discount is calculated on the reduced price, not on the original marked price.

For successive discounts of a% and b%, the equivalent single discount is:

Equivalent discount = a + b − ab/100.

Worked example: An item receives discounts of 20% and 10%.

  1. Equivalent discount = 20 + 10 − (20 × 10)/100.
  2. Equivalent discount = 30 − 2 = 28%.

A 28% discount is equivalent to those two successive discounts.

9. Markup and discount together

Sometimes a seller increases the cost price to establish a marked price and then gives a discount. The final profit or loss must be calculated by comparing the actual selling price with the original cost price.

Worked example: An item costs ₹500. Its marked price is 40% above cost, and a 10% discount is offered.

  1. Marked price = 500 × 1.40 = ₹700.
  2. Discount = 10% of 700 = ₹70.
  3. Selling price = 700 − 70 = ₹630.
  4. Profit = 630 − 500 = ₹130.
  5. Profit percentage = (130/500) × 100 = 26%.
10. Successive percentage changes

Repeated increases and decreases must be applied to the current value at each stage.

For two successive changes a% and b%, using a negative sign for a decrease:

Net change = a + b + ab/100.

Example: A price increases by 20% and then decreases by 20%.

Net change = 20 − 20 + (20 × −20)/100 = −4%.

Therefore, the final price is 4% lower than the original price.

11. Profit and loss when quantities differ

Some questions compare the cost of several items with their combined selling price. First calculate the total cost and total selling price, then calculate profit or loss on the total transaction.

Do not average individual profit percentages directly unless their cost prices are equal. The correct overall percentage uses the total cost price as the base.

12. False weights and effective profit

Some aptitude problems describe a seller charging the cost price while supplying less than the stated quantity. The effective profit comes from the difference between the quantity paid for and the quantity supplied.

Illustration: If a seller charges for 1 kg but supplies only 900 g, the buyer receives 90% of the promised quantity. Compare the amount received with the actual quantity supplied to determine the effective rate or profit.

Always identify the actual quantity and the quantity used for pricing before calculating.

13. Important relationships and checks
  • Profit and loss percentages use CP as the base.
  • Discount percentage uses MP as the base.
  • SP = CP + Profit.
  • SP = CP − Loss.
  • SP = MP − Discount.
  • A discount reduces the marked price; it does not automatically imply a loss.
  • A seller can offer a discount and still make a profit if SP remains above CP.
14. Common mistakes
  • Calculating profit percentage using selling price instead of cost price.
  • Calculating discount percentage using cost price instead of marked price.
  • Subtracting successive discounts directly.
  • Assuming a discount always means a loss.
  • Mixing up marked price, cost price and selling price.
  • Ignoring the original base when comparing percentage changes.
15. Solving difficult commercial arithmetic questions
  1. Write down the known values: CP, SP, MP, profit, loss or discount.
  2. Identify which value is the base for the requested percentage.
  3. Convert the percentage into a multiplier or fraction.
  4. Apply successive changes in the correct order.
  5. Calculate the final selling price or profit/loss.
  6. Check whether the result is reasonable by comparing CP, MP and SP.
Important formulas and quick reference
  • Profit = SP - CP.
  • Loss = CP - SP.
  • Profit% = Profit/CP × 100.
  • Loss% = Loss/CP × 100.
  • Discount = MP - SP.
Worked example

An item costs ₹500 and sells for ₹600.

  1. Profit = 600 - 500 = ₹100.
  2. Profit percentage = 100/500 × 100.
  3. Profit = 20%.

Chapter 1: Ancient, Medieval and Modern Indian History

1. Understanding historical periods

Indian history is generally studied through Ancient, Medieval and Modern periods. Study each period through its major developments, rulers, institutions, cultural contributions and important events.

Ancient India

  • Indus Valley Civilisation: Known for planned settlements, drainage systems, trade, craft production and standardised weights. Important sites include Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Dholavira and Lothal.
  • Vedic Period: The Rigveda is the earliest Veda. The Vedic literature provides information about early society, religious practices and political organisation.
  • Mahajanapadas: The period saw the emergence of sixteen major political territories, including Magadha, Kosala and Avanti.
  • Buddhism: Founded by Gautama Buddha. Its central teachings include the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path.

Chapter 2: Indian and World Geography

1. Earth and its movements

Earth is approximately spherical and rotates on its axis while revolving around the Sun.

  • Rotation: Earth's rotation causes day and night.
  • Revolution: Earth's movement around the Sun, combined with its axial tilt, produces seasonal changes.
  • Latitude: Angular distance north or south of the Equator.
  • Longitude: Angular distance east or west of the Prime Meridian.
  • Equator: The 0° latitude line dividing Earth into Northern and Southern Hemispheres.
  • Prime Meridian: The 0° longitude reference line passing through Greenwich.

Atmosphere and climate

The atmosphere is the gaseous envelope surrounding Earth. Its major layers are the troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere and exosphere.

  • Weather: Short-term atmospheric conditions at a particular place.
  • Climate: Long-term patterns and averages of weather.
  • Monsoon: A seasonal reversal in wind patterns that strongly influences rainfall in South Asia.
  • Humidity: The amount of water vapour present in the air.
  • Pressure: The force exerted by the atmosphere per unit area.

Physical divisions of India

  • Himalayan Mountains: Young fold mountains in northern India.
  • Northern Plains: Fertile plains formed largely by deposits from the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra river systems.
  • Peninsular Plateau: An ancient landmass containing important mineral resources.
  • Indian Desert: The Thar Desert is mainly located in Rajasthan.
  • Coastal Plains: The western and eastern coastal regions have different geographical characteristics.
  • Islands: Andaman and Nicobar Islands are in the Bay of Bengal; Lakshadweep is in the Arabian Sea.

Rivers and drainage

The Himalayan river systems include the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra. Important Peninsular rivers include Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri, Mahanadi, Narmada and Tapi.

The Narmada and Tapi generally flow westwards. Many major Peninsular rivers flow eastwards into the Bay of Bengal.

Resources and agriculture

  • Alluvial soil: Common in river plains and generally suitable for agriculture.
  • Black soil: Known for moisture retention and its association with cotton cultivation.
  • Red soil: Develops from weathering of crystalline rocks and often has low natural fertility.
  • Kharif crops: Generally grown with the monsoon, such as rice and maize.
  • Rabi crops: Generally grown during the winter season, such as wheat and mustard.

Chapter 3: General Science — Physics, Chemistry and Biology

Part A: Physics

Physics explains matter, energy, motion and the interactions between physical objects.

  • Motion: Change in position with time.
  • Speed: Distance travelled per unit time.
  • Velocity: Displacement per unit time, including direction.
  • Acceleration: Rate of change of velocity.
  • Force: An interaction that can change an object's motion or shape. SI unit: newton (N).
  • Work: Work is done when a force produces displacement in its direction. For constant force, \(W=Fs\cos\theta\).
  • Power: Rate of doing work. SI unit: watt (W).
  • Energy: Capacity to do work. SI unit: joule (J).

Heat, light and sound

  • Conduction: Transfer of heat through direct contact.
  • Convection: Heat transfer through movement of fluids.
  • Radiation: Heat transfer through electromagnetic waves.
  • Reflection: Bouncing back of light from a surface.
  • Refraction: Change in direction of light when it passes between media with different optical properties.
  • Frequency: Number of oscillations per second, measured in hertz.
  • Sound: A mechanical wave that requires a medium to travel.

Electricity and magnetism

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge. Voltage is electrical potential difference and resistance opposes current.

Ohm's law: \(V=IR\), where V is voltage, I is current and R is resistance, under suitable constant physical conditions.

Electric power can be calculated using \(P=VI\). Electrical energy depends on power and time.

Part B: Chemistry

  • Atom: Basic unit of an element, containing protons, neutrons and electrons.
  • Element: Pure substance consisting of atoms with the same atomic number.
  • Compound: Substance formed when elements combine chemically in fixed proportions.
  • Mixture: Physical combination of substances that retain their chemical identities.
  • Acid: A substance that increases hydrogen-ion concentration in aqueous solution under the usual introductory model.
  • Base: A substance that accepts protons or increases hydroxide-ion concentration in aqueous solution, depending on the model used.
  • Oxidation: Loss of electrons in the electron-transfer definition.
  • Reduction: Gain of electrons in the electron-transfer definition.

The periodic table arranges elements by increasing atomic number. Elements in the same group often have similar chemical properties because of their valence-electron configurations.

Part C: Biology

  • Cell: Basic structural and functional unit of life.
  • Nucleus: Contains most of the cell's genetic material in eukaryotic cells.
  • Mitochondria: Major site of ATP production during aerobic cellular respiration.
  • Chloroplast: Site of photosynthesis in plants and algae.
  • Blood: Transports gases, nutrients, hormones and waste products.
  • Heart: Muscular organ that pumps blood through the circulatory system.
  • Kidneys: Filter blood and help regulate water and electrolyte balance.
  • Genetics: Study of heredity and variation.

Everyday science

Understand common scientific phenomena such as why the sky appears blue, why metals expand on heating, how a refrigerator transfers heat, why objects float and how vaccines help the immune system develop protection.

Chapter 1: Understanding Ratios

1. What is a ratio?

A ratio compares two quantities of the same kind by division. It tells us how large one quantity is in relation to another.

If a class has 20 boys and 15 girls, the ratio of boys to girls is 20 : 15, which simplifies to 4 : 3.

2. Writing and simplifying ratios

A ratio a : b can also be written as a/b, where b is not zero. Divide both terms by their highest common factor to obtain the simplest form.

Example: Simplify 36 : 48.

The HCF of 36 and 48 is 12. Dividing both terms by 12 gives 3 : 4.

3. Important properties

  • Order matters: 2 : 3 is not the same as 3 : 2.
  • Equivalent ratios: Multiplying or dividing both terms by the same non-zero number does not change the ratio.
  • Same units: Convert quantities into the same unit before comparing them.
  • Zero denominator: The second term of a ratio cannot be zero when written as a fraction.

4. Solved examples

Easy: Find the ratio of 2 metres to 50 centimetres.

Convert 2 metres into 200 centimetres.

Ratio = 200 : 50 = 4 : 1.

Moderate: Divide ₹840 in the ratio 3 : 4.

Total parts = 3 + 4 = 7. One part = 840/7 = ₹120.

Shares = 3 × 120 = ₹360 and 4 × 120 = ₹480.

Difficult: Two quantities are in the ratio 5 : 7. If their difference is 36, find them.

Difference in parts = 7 − 5 = 2. One part = 36/2 = 18.

Quantities = 5 × 18 = 90 and 7 × 18 = 126.

5. Practice questions

  1. Simplify 42 : 56.
  2. Find the ratio of 3 hours to 45 minutes.
  3. Divide ₹960 in the ratio 5 : 7.
Reveal answers with explanations

1. 3 : 4, after dividing both terms by 14. 2. 180 : 45 = 4 : 1. 3. Total parts = 12; one part = ₹80. Shares = ₹400 and ₹560.

Chapter 2: Equivalent Ratios and Compound Ratios

1. Equivalent ratios

Two ratios are equivalent when they represent the same comparison. For example, 2 : 3, 4 : 6 and 10 : 15 are equivalent.

If a : b = c : d, then ad = bc, provided the denominators are non-zero.

2. Finding a missing term

Suppose 5 : 8 = 20 : x.

Cross multiplication gives 5x = 8 × 20 = 160. Therefore, x = 32.

3. Compound ratio

The compound ratio of a : b and c : d is ac : bd. Multiply the antecedents together and the consequents together.

Example: The compound ratio of 2 : 3 and 4 : 5 is 8 : 15.

4. Dividing a quantity using equivalent ratios

When several ratios describe the same set of quantities, first make the common terms equal and then combine the ratios.

Example: If A : B = 2 : 3 and B : C = 4 : 5, make B equal to 12.

A : B = 8 : 12 and B : C = 12 : 15. Therefore, A : B : C = 8 : 12 : 15.

5. Practice questions

  1. Find x if 7 : 9 = 21 : x.
  2. Find the compound ratio of 3 : 4 and 5 : 6.
  3. If A : B = 3 : 5 and B : C = 10 : 7, find A : B : C.
Reveal answers with explanations

1. 7x = 189, so x = 27. 2. 15 : 24 = 5 : 8. 3. A : B = 6 : 10 and B : C = 10 : 7, so the combined ratio is 6 : 10 : 7.

Chapter 3: Proportion and Its Properties

1. Meaning of proportion

Four quantities a, b, c and d are in proportion when a : b = c : d. It is written as a : b :: c : d.

Here, a and d are called extremes, while b and c are called means.

2. Fundamental rule

If a : b = c : d, then:

ad = bc

This relationship is useful for finding an unknown term in a proportion.

3. Important transformations

  • Invertendo: If a/b = c/d, then b/a = d/c.
  • Alternendo: a/c = b/d.
  • Componendo: (a + b)/b = (c + d)/d.
  • Dividendo: (a − b)/b = (c − d)/d.
  • Componendo and dividendo: (a + b)/(a − b) = (c + d)/(c − d), where the denominators are non-zero.

4. Solved examples

Easy: Find x if 4 : 7 = x : 21.

4/7 = x/21. Therefore, 7x = 84 and x = 12.

Moderate: If 6 : 9 = 10 : x, find x.

6x = 90, so x = 15.

Difficult: If a/b = 5/3, find (a + b)/(a − b).

Put a = 5k and b = 3k. The required ratio is 8k : 2k = 4 : 1.

5. Practice questions

  1. Find x if 8 : 12 = x : 30.
  2. If 9 : 15 = 12 : x, find x.
  3. If a/b = 7/4, find (a + b)/(a − b).
Reveal answers with explanations

1. 20. 2. 20, using 9x = 180. 3. (7 + 4)/(7 − 4) = 11 : 3.

Chapter 4: Continued Proportion and Mean Proportional

1. Continued proportion

Three quantities a, b and c are in continued proportion when a : b = b : c.

Cross multiplication gives b² = ac.

2. Mean proportional

The mean proportional between two positive quantities a and c is the number b satisfying a : b = b : c.

b = √(ac)

For example, the mean proportional between 4 and 9 is √36 = 6.

3. Third proportional

If a : b = b : c, then c is the third proportional to a and b.

c = b²/a

4. Fourth proportional

If a : b = c : d, then d is the fourth proportional to a, b and c.

d = bc/a

5. Solved examples

Easy: Find the mean proportional between 9 and 16.

√(9 × 16) = √144 = 12.

Moderate: Find the third proportional to 6 and 12.

c = 12²/6 = 24.

Difficult: Find the fourth proportional to 4, 10 and 14.

4 : 10 = 14 : d. Therefore, 4d = 140 and d = 35.

6. Practice questions

  1. Find the mean proportional between 5 and 20.
  2. Find the third proportional to 8 and 12.
  3. Find the fourth proportional to 6, 9 and 14.
Reveal answers with explanations

1. √100 = 10. 2. 12²/8 = 18. 3. 6 : 9 = 14 : d, so d = 21.

Chapter 5: Direct Variation

1. Meaning of direct variation

Two quantities vary directly when an increase or decrease in one quantity produces the same proportional change in the other, provided the relationship remains constant.

For example, at a fixed price per notebook, the total cost increases directly with the number of notebooks purchased.

2. Formula

y ∝ x

y = kx

Here, k is the constant of proportionality. It is equal to y/x.

3. Solved examples

Easy: If 5 pens cost ₹60, find the cost of 8 pens.

Cost per pen = 60/5 = ₹12. Cost of 8 pens = ₹96.

Moderate: A machine produces 240 units in 4 hours at a constant rate. How many units will it produce in 7 hours?

Production per hour = 240/4 = 60. In 7 hours = 420 units.

Difficult: y varies directly as x. If y = 42 when x = 6, find y when x = 11.

k = 42/6 = 7. Therefore, y = 7 × 11 = 77.

4. Important checks

  • Identify whether the rate or unit value remains constant.
  • Keep the units consistent.
  • Do not assume direct variation if the problem does not establish a constant relationship.

5. Practice questions

  1. If 7 kg of rice costs ₹350, find the cost of 12 kg.
  2. If y varies directly as x and y = 24 when x = 8, find y when x = 15.
  3. A vehicle covers 180 km in 3 hours at a constant speed. How far will it travel in 5 hours?
Reveal answers with explanations

1. ₹50 per kg, so ₹600. 2. k = 3, so y = 45. 3. Speed = 60 km/h, so distance = 300 km.

Chapter 6: Inverse Variation

1. Meaning of inverse variation

Two quantities vary inversely when one increases in the same proportion as the other decreases, so their product remains constant.

For a fixed amount of work, more workers generally require less time when all workers work at the same rate.

2. Formula

y ∝ 1/x

xy = k

x₁y₁ = x₂y₂

Here, k is the constant product.

3. Solved examples

Easy: Six workers complete a task in 12 days. How long would 8 equally efficient workers take?

6 × 12 = 8 × d. Therefore, d = 72/8 = 9 days.

Moderate: A journey takes 5 hours at 60 km/h. How long will it take at 75 km/h for the same distance?

Distance = 60 × 5 = 300 km. Time = 300/75 = 4 hours.

Difficult: Fifteen taps fill a tank in 8 hours. How long will 20 identical taps take, assuming equal flow and no other changes?

15 × 8 = 20 × t. Therefore, t = 6 hours.

4. Important limitation

Inverse variation applies only when the relevant total remains fixed and the rate or working conditions are unchanged.

5. Practice questions

  1. Ten workers complete a task in 18 days. How many days will 15 equally efficient workers take?
  2. A car takes 6 hours at 50 km/h. Find the time at 75 km/h for the same journey.
  3. If x and y vary inversely and y = 12 when x = 5, find y when x = 15.
Reveal answers with explanations

1. 10 × 18/15 = 12 days. 2. Distance = 300 km; time = 4 hours. 3. xy = 60, so y = 4.

Chapter 7: Compound and Joint Variation

1. Compound variation

A quantity may depend directly on one variable and inversely on another. This is called compound variation.

For example, the time needed to complete a fixed amount of work varies inversely with the number of workers, if their individual working rates are equal.

2. Joint variation

A quantity varies jointly with two or more variables when it is directly proportional to their product.

z ∝ xy

z = kxy

3. Combined direct and inverse variation

z = kx/y

In this relationship, z varies directly as x and inversely as y.

4. Solved examples

Easy: z varies jointly as x and y. If z = 24 when x = 3 and y = 4, find k.

24 = k × 3 × 4. Therefore, k = 2.

Moderate: z varies directly as x and inversely as y. If z = 10 when x = 5 and y = 2, find z when x = 9 and y = 3.

k = zy/x = 10 × 2/5 = 4. New z = 4 × 9/3 = 12.

Difficult: The volume of a cuboid varies jointly with its length, breadth and height. If its dimensions are doubled, what happens to its volume?

New volume factor = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 times the original volume.

5. Practice questions

  1. If z = kxy, z = 60, x = 5 and y = 3, find k.
  2. If z varies as x/y and k = 6, x = 8 and y = 4, find z.
  3. If all three dimensions of a cuboid are tripled, by what factor does its volume change?
Reveal answers with explanations

1. k = 60/15 = 4. 2. z = 6 × 8/4 = 12. 3. 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 times.

Chapter 8: Partnership and Profit Sharing

1. Meaning of partnership

A partnership is a business arrangement in which two or more people contribute capital and share the resulting profit or loss according to agreed conditions.

2. Basic rule

When partners invest for the same duration, profit is divided in the ratio of their investments.

When investment periods differ, the relevant comparison is based on capital multiplied by time.

Profit share ratio = Capital × Time

3. Solved examples

Easy: A and B invest ₹20,000 and ₹30,000 for one year. Divide a profit of ₹10,000.

Investment ratio = 2 : 3. A receives ₹4,000 and B receives ₹6,000.

Moderate: A invests ₹12,000 for 12 months and B invests ₹18,000 for 8 months. Find their profit ratio.

A : B = 12,000 × 12 : 18,000 × 8 = 144,000 : 144,000 = 1 : 1.

Difficult: A invests ₹40,000 for 12 months. B joins after 4 months with ₹60,000 and invests for the remaining 8 months. Divide a profit of ₹18,000.

A : B = 40,000 × 12 : 60,000 × 8 = 480,000 : 480,000 = 1 : 1.

Each receives ₹9,000.

4. Important points

  • Count the actual number of months each partner invests.
  • Include additional capital introduced later, if the question specifies it.
  • Share losses using the same agreed ratio unless the question gives another arrangement.

5. Practice questions

  1. A and B invest ₹15,000 and ₹25,000 for equal periods. Divide a profit of ₹8,000.
  2. A invests ₹10,000 for 12 months and B invests ₹15,000 for 8 months. Find their profit ratio.
  3. A invests ₹30,000 for 12 months and B invests ₹40,000 for 6 months. Divide a profit of ₹12,000.
Reveal answers with explanations

1. Ratio 3 : 5; shares ₹3,000 and ₹5,000. 2. 120,000 : 120,000 = 1 : 1. 3. Ratio 360,000 : 240,000 = 3 : 2; shares ₹7,200 and ₹4,800.

Chapter 9: Ratio-Based Age and Mixture Problems

1. Age problems using ratios

When present ages are in a ratio a : b, represent them as ax and bx. Use the given difference or total to calculate x.

When years are added to or subtracted from ages, update each age before forming the new ratio.

2. Solved age examples

Easy: Two ages are in the ratio 3 : 5 and their sum is 40 years.

8x = 40, so x = 5. Ages are 15 years and 25 years.

Moderate: The present ages of A and B are in the ratio 4 : 5. Their difference is 8 years. Find their ages.

x = 8, so their ages are 32 years and 40 years.

Difficult: The present ages of two people are in the ratio 2 : 3. After 10 years, their ages will be in the ratio 3 : 4. Find their present ages.

Let the ages be 2x and 3x.

(2x + 10)/(3x + 10) = 3/4.

8x + 40 = 9x + 30, so x = 10. Their present ages are 20 years and 30 years.

3. Mixture problems

Mixture questions often compare quantities of two ingredients. If the quantities are mixed, the resulting ratio is found by adding the amounts of each ingredient separately.

Example: A container has 2 litres of milk and 3 litres of water. The milk-to-water ratio is 2 : 3.

4. Repeated addition and removal

When a part of a mixture is removed and replaced, the remaining quantities change. Carefully identify the total mixture, the fraction removed and the amount replaced before forming the new ratio.

5. Practice questions

  1. Two ages are in the ratio 5 : 7 and their sum is 48. Find the ages.
  2. The ages of A and B are in the ratio 3 : 4. Their difference is 6 years. Find their ages.
  3. A mixture contains 12 litres of milk and 8 litres of water. Find the ratio of milk to water.
Reveal answers with explanations

1. 12 parts = 48, so ages are 20 and 28. 2. One part = 6, so ages are 18 and 24. 3. 12 : 8 = 3 : 2.

Chapter 10: Mixed Ratio and Proportion Problems

1. A systematic method

  1. Identify the quantities being compared.
  2. Convert all measurements into consistent units.
  3. Determine whether the relationship is direct, inverse or a fixed ratio.
  4. Represent unknown quantities using variables.
  5. Form an equation using the given condition.
  6. Solve and check whether the answer satisfies the original condition.

2. Common examination traps

  • Reversing the order of a ratio.
  • Comparing quantities without converting units.
  • Using direct proportion in a fixed-work problem where inverse proportion is required.
  • Ignoring the duration of investment in partnership questions.
  • Adding ratio terms incorrectly when dividing a total quantity.

3. Higher-level solved examples

Moderate: A sum is divided among A, B and C in the ratio 2 : 3 : 5. If C receives ₹1,500, find the total sum.

Five parts = ₹1,500, so one part = ₹300. Total = 10 × ₹300 = ₹3,000.

Difficult: A and B have incomes in the ratio 5 : 7 and expenditures in the ratio 3 : 4. If both save ₹2,000, find their incomes.

Let incomes be 5x and 7x, and expenditures be 3y and 4y.

5x − 3y = 2,000 and 7x − 4y = 2,000.

Multiplying the first equation by 4 and the second by 3 gives 20x − 12y = 8,000 and 21x − 12y = 6,000. Subtracting gives x = −2,000, which would imply negative incomes.

Conclusion: The given ratios and equal positive savings are inconsistent. Always check whether the conditions of a word problem can hold together.

4. Chapter-end practice test

  2. Find x if 12 : 18 = 20 : x.
  3. Find the mean proportional between 16 and 25.
  4. If 12 workers complete a task in 15 days, how many days will 20 equally efficient workers take?
  5. A and B invest ₹25,000 and ₹35,000 for equal periods. Divide a profit of ₹12,000.
  6. Two numbers are in the ratio 7 : 9 and their difference is 18. Find the numbers.
  7. If y varies directly as x and y = 36 when x = 9, find y when x = 14.
  8. If x and y vary inversely and x = 8, y = 15, find y when x = 12.
Reveal test answers with explanations

1. 2 : 3. 2. 30, since 12x = 360. 3. √400 = 20. 4. 12 × 15/20 = 9 days. 5. Ratio 5 : 7; shares ₹5,000 and ₹7,000. 6. Difference is 2 parts, so one part is 9; numbers are 63 and 81. 7. k = 4, so y = 56. 8. xy = 120, so y = 10.

Chapter 1: Fundamentals of Time and Work

1. Understanding work

Time and Work problems study the relationship between the amount of work completed, the time taken and the rate at which a person or machine works.

If a person completes a task in 10 days while working at a constant rate, the person completes one-tenth of the work each day.

2. Basic work formula

Work = Efficiency × Time

Efficiency = Work / Time

Time = Work / Efficiency

Efficiency means the amount of work completed per unit of time. A higher efficiency means less time is required for the same task.

3. Representing total work

For calculation convenience, total work can be represented by the LCM of the individual completion times.

If A completes a task in 6 days and B in 8 days, take total work as LCM(6, 8) = 24 units.

  • A's daily work = 24/6 = 4 units.
  • B's daily work = 24/8 = 3 units.

4. Solved examples

Easy: A completes a task in 12 days. What fraction of the work does A complete in one day?

Daily work = 1/12 of the task.

Moderate: A completes a task in 15 days. How much work does A complete in 5 days?

Work completed = 5/15 = 1/3 of the task.

Difficult: A machine produces 480 components in 8 hours. How many components will it produce in 13 hours at the same rate?

Hourly production = 480/8 = 60. Total production = 60 × 13 = 780 components.

5. Practice questions

  1. A completes a task in 20 days. What fraction does A complete in one day?
  2. A completes 3/5 of a task in 6 days. How long will the complete task take at the same rate?
  3. A machine produces 360 units in 6 hours. Find its hourly production.
Reveal answers with explanations

1. 1/20. 2. Daily work = 3/30 = 1/10, so 10 days. 3. 360/6 = 60 units per hour.

Chapter 2: Individual Efficiency and Time Relationship

1. Time and efficiency are inversely related

For the same fixed amount of work, a person who works faster takes less time. Therefore, efficiency and completion time are inversely proportional.

Efficiency ∝ 1 / Time

Efficiency ratio = Inverse of time ratio

2. Comparing two workers

If A completes a task in 10 days and B completes the same task in 15 days, their efficiencies are in the ratio:

Efficiency of A : Efficiency of B = 1/10 : 1/15 = 3 : 2.

3. Percentage change in efficiency

If efficiency increases by 25%, the new efficiency becomes 125% of the original. The new time is therefore 100/125 of the original time.

For a 25% efficiency increase, the time decreases by 20%, not 25%.

4. Solved examples

Easy: A takes 8 days and B takes 12 days to complete the same work. Find their efficiency ratio.

1/8 : 1/12 = 3 : 2.

Moderate: A is twice as efficient as B. If B takes 18 days, how long does A take?

A takes half the time = 9 days.

Difficult: A worker's efficiency increases by 20%. If the original completion time was 30 days, find the new time.

New time = 30 × 100/120 = 25 days.

5. Practice questions

  1. A takes 9 days and B takes 12 days. Find their efficiency ratio.
  2. A is three times as efficient as B. If B takes 24 days, find A's time.
  3. Efficiency increases by 50%. If the original time was 36 days, find the new time.
Reveal answers with explanations

1. 1/9 : 1/12 = 4 : 3. 2. 24/3 = 8 days. 3. 36 × 100/150 = 24 days.

Chapter 3: Working Together and Combined Work

1. Combined work

When two or more people work together, their daily work rates are added, provided they work independently and do not interfere with each other.

Combined daily work = A's daily work + B's daily work

Combined time = 1 / Combined daily work

2. Formula for two workers

If A completes a task in x days and B in y days, their combined completion time is:

Combined time = xy / (x + y)

3. Solved examples

Easy: A completes work in 10 days and B in 10 days. Find their combined time.

Combined time = (10 × 10)/(10 + 10) = 5 days.

Moderate: A takes 12 days and B takes 18 days. Find the time taken together.

Combined time = (12 × 18)/(12 + 18) = 216/30 = 7.2 days.

Difficult: A and B together complete a task in 8 days. A alone takes 12 days. Find B's individual time.

B's daily work = 1/8 − 1/12 = 1/24. Therefore, B takes 24 days.

4. Three or more workers

For three workers, add all three individual daily work rates. For example, if their daily rates are 1/6, 1/8 and 1/12, their combined rate is 3/8 of the task per day.

5. Practice questions

  1. A takes 15 days and B takes 10 days. Find their combined time.
  2. A and B together finish work in 6 days. A alone takes 10 days. Find B's time.
  3. A, B and C complete a task individually in 12, 15 and 20 days. Find their combined time.
Reveal answers with explanations

1. 150/25 = 6 days. 2. B's rate = 1/6 − 1/10 = 1/15, so 15 days. 3. Combined rate = 1/12 + 1/15 + 1/20 = 1/5, so 5 days.

Chapter 4: Alternate and Partial Working

1. Alternate-day working

Some questions require workers to work on alternate days. In such cases, calculate the work completed during each complete working cycle before calculating the remaining work.

2. Solved examples

Easy: A completes 1/6 of a task per day and B completes 1/3 per day. If they work on alternate days, starting with A, how much is completed in two days?

Two-day work = 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/2.

Moderate: A can complete a task in 6 days and B in 12 days. They work on alternate days, starting with A. Find the completion time.

Daily rates are 1/6 and 1/12. Two-day work = 1/4. In 6 days, three cycles complete 3/4 of the task. On day 7, A completes the remaining 1/4 of the task at 1/6 per day, requiring 1.5 days.

Total time = 7.5 days.

Difficult: A and B complete a task in 10 and 15 days respectively. A works alone for 2 days, after which B joins. Find the total time.

A completes 2/10 = 1/5. Remaining work = 4/5. Combined daily work = 1/10 + 1/15 = 1/6.

Time for remaining work = (4/5)/(1/6) = 24/5 = 4.8 days. Total time = 6.8 days.

3. Partial work and remaining work

When a worker joins or leaves, calculate the completed work first. Subtract it from the total and use the new combined rate for the remaining task.

4. Practice questions

  1. A completes 1/8 of a task per day and B completes 1/4. Find the work completed in two days if they alternate, starting with A.
  2. A completes a task in 8 days and B in 12 days. A works for 2 days alone, then B joins. Find the total completion time.
  3. A and B take 10 and 20 days individually. They alternate days, starting with A. Find the completion time.
Reveal answers with explanations

1. 1/8 + 1/4 = 3/8. 2. A completes 1/4; remaining work is 3/4. Combined rate = 5/24, so remaining time = 18/5 = 3.6 days. Total = 5.6 days. 3. Two-day work = 1/10 + 1/20 = 3/20. Six complete cycles finish 18/20 in 12 days; the remaining 1/10 takes 1 day on A's turn. Total = 13 days.

Chapter 5: Wages and Work Distribution

1. How wages are divided

When people are paid according to the amount of work completed, their wages are divided in the ratio of their actual work contributions.

If workers have different efficiencies and work for different durations, compare efficiency multiplied by time.

Work contribution = Efficiency × Time

Wage ratio = Work contribution ratio

2. Solved examples

Easy: A and B complete equal amounts of work. The total payment is ₹6,000. Find each person's share.

Equal contribution means equal payment. Each receives ₹3,000.

Moderate: A and B have efficiencies in the ratio 2 : 3 and work for the same duration. Divide ₹10,000.

Wage ratio = 2 : 3. Shares are ₹4,000 and ₹6,000.

Difficult: A works at 4 units per day for 6 days, while B works at 6 units per day for 4 days. Divide ₹7,000 according to work.

A's work = 24 units. B's work = 24 units. Each receives ₹3,500.

3. Common mistakes

  • Using time alone when efficiencies are different.
  • Dividing wages equally when contributions are unequal.
  • Ignoring a change in working duration.

4. Practice questions

  1. A and B work at efficiencies 3 : 5 for equal periods. Divide ₹16,000.
  2. A works at 5 units per day for 8 days and B at 4 units per day for 10 days. Divide ₹9,000.
  3. Two workers contribute 30 and 45 units of work. Divide ₹6,000.
Reveal answers with explanations

1. ₹6,000 and ₹10,000. 2. Both complete 40 units, so each receives ₹4,500. 3. Ratio 2 : 3; shares ₹2,400 and ₹3,600.

Chapter 6: Pipes and Cisterns — Filling a Tank

1. Understanding the problem

Pipes and Cisterns questions apply the same work-rate principles. A filling pipe adds water to a tank, while an emptying pipe removes water.

If a pipe fills a tank in x hours, its filling rate is 1/x of the tank per hour.

2. Combined filling pipes

Combined filling rate = 1/x + 1/y

Time = 1 / Combined rate

3. Solved examples

Easy: A pipe fills a tank in 6 hours. What fraction is filled in one hour?

1/6 of the tank.

Moderate: Two pipes fill a tank in 8 and 12 hours. Find the time taken together.

Combined rate = 1/8 + 1/12 = 5/24. Time = 24/5 = 4.8 hours.

Difficult: Three pipes fill a tank in 6, 8 and 12 hours. Find the time taken together.

Combined rate = 1/6 + 1/8 + 1/12 = 9/24 = 3/8. Time = 8/3 hours, or 2 hours 40 minutes.

4. Converting units

One hour contains 60 minutes. Convert fractional hours into minutes by multiplying the fractional part by 60.

5. Practice questions

  1. Two pipes fill a tank in 10 and 15 hours. Find their combined time.
  2. Three pipes fill a tank in 4, 6 and 12 hours. Find their combined time.
  3. A pipe fills 3/5 of a tank in 6 hours. Find the time required to fill the entire tank at the same rate.
Reveal answers with explanations

1. Rate = 1/10 + 1/15 = 1/6, so 6 hours. 2. Rate = 1/4 + 1/6 + 1/12 = 1/2, so 2 hours. 3. 6 × 5/3 = 10 hours.

Chapter 7: Emptying Pipes, Leaks and Net Work

1. Emptying pipes

An emptying pipe reduces the amount of water in a tank. Its rate is treated as negative when combined with filling pipes.

Net rate = Filling rate − Emptying rate

2. Solved examples

Easy: A pipe fills a tank in 6 hours and another empties it in 12 hours. Find the net filling rate.

Net rate = 1/6 − 1/12 = 1/12 tank per hour.

Moderate: A filling pipe fills a tank in 8 hours, while a leak empties it in 24 hours. Find the time to fill the tank when both are open.

Net rate = 1/8 − 1/24 = 1/12. Time = 12 hours.

Difficult: Two pipes fill a tank in 10 and 15 hours, while a third empties it in 30 hours. Find the net filling time when all are open.

Net rate = 1/10 + 1/15 − 1/30 = 1/6. Time = 6 hours.

3. Important cases

  • If the net rate is positive, the tank fills.
  • If the net rate is negative, the tank empties.
  • If the net rate is zero, the water level remains unchanged under the assumed constant rates.

4. Practice questions

  1. A pipe fills a tank in 5 hours and a leak empties it in 20 hours. Find the net filling time.
  2. Two pipes fill a tank in 12 and 18 hours. A leak empties it in 36 hours. Find the time when all are open.
  3. A pipe fills a tank in 4 hours and an outlet empties it in 6 hours. Find the net rate and determine whether the tank fills or empties.
Reveal answers with explanations

1. Net rate = 1/5 − 1/20 = 3/20; time = 20/3 hours. 2. Net rate = 1/12 + 1/18 − 1/36 = 1/9; time = 9 hours. 3. Net rate = 1/4 − 1/6 = 1/12 tank per hour; the tank fills.

Chapter 8: Mixed Problems and Chapter-End Test

1. A reliable problem-solving method

  1. Identify the total work or total tank capacity.
  2. Convert individual completion times into rates.
  3. Add rates for simultaneous work and subtract rates for emptying or removal.
  4. Account for the exact days or hours each person or pipe operates.
  5. Calculate the remaining work before changing the working arrangement.
  6. Check that the final time and work amount are consistent.

2. Frequently tested distinctions

  • Time versus efficiency: They are inversely related for fixed work.
  • Combined work: Independent positive work rates are added.
  • Leak: Its rate is subtracted from the filling rate.
  • Wages: Payment follows actual work contribution when the question specifies payment by work.
  • Alternate work: Calculate complete cycles and then handle the remaining work.

3. Higher-level solved examples

Moderate: A can complete a task in 20 days and B in 30 days. They work together for 6 days. What fraction remains?

Combined rate = 1/20 + 1/30 = 1/12. Work completed = 6/12 = 1/2. Remaining work = 1/2.

Difficult: A and B together complete a task in 6 days. B and C take 8 days, while A and C take 12 days. Find the time taken by all three together.

Adding the three pair rates gives 2(A + B + C) = 1/6 + 1/8 + 1/12 = 3/8.

Combined rate = 3/16. Therefore, all three together take 16/3 days.
